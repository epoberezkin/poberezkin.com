Can AI Agents Give Us the Internet Back?

Posted on October 20, 2026 by Evgeny Poberezkin
Tags: ai, internet

They can – but not on the networks most people use.

Internet: a market that nobody owned

When the internet began, only research and education were allowed on it. In 1992 Congress opened it to commerce, and in 1993 CERN gave up all rights to the web’s code1.

Anybody could build on it, publish and read, and people found services in catalogs2.

How we lost it to big tech

Catalogs were curated manually and could not keep up as the web grew. Search replaced them, and a few companies came to own it. And software was expensive to build at scale, so well-funded companies built the services most people used.

The market became a few walled gardens. Their owners decide who gets found and charge for it3, see every buyer and take a cut of the deals.

AI changes the game

AI can solve the three problems that closed the internet market:

  • catalogs can scale again, because AI-powered services can curate and search them;
  • search indices can become a public good, because data is now cheap;
  • creating software costs a fraction of what it did, so anybody can build a service.

Agents can also make buying cheaper, safer and more convenient: an agent can compare a thousand times more offers than a person, without an aggregator skewing the comparison toward whoever pays; it can protect its owner from manipulation; and it gives everybody what only a few could afford – a personal concierge.

But AI also breaks trust in the networks we use. It can clone a voice and a face, and phone numbers and email addresses are easy to spoof. Anybody who has your number or email can target you, or pretend to be somebody you know – and an agent can be deceived the same way.

What keeps a market open

A market stays open only while three conditions hold:

  1. Sellers can be found and trusted. When anybody can be faked, a seller’s reputation should be attached to a trusted name, or agents will buy from impostors.
  2. Buyers can ask without being identified. A request tells who wants what, when and for how much. Whoever learns that can raise the price for the buyer who needs it most4.
  3. Nobody sits in between. A middleman decides who gets found and takes a cut of every deal – this is how the open market closed last time.

Why today’s solutions fail

Platforms. We saw what happens when a platform owns the market: it decides who gets found, sees every buyer and takes its cut. Agents cannot fix it. Meta’s business agents, for example, already sell inside WhatsApp and Messenger5, where Meta knows who buys what.

Websites with MCP services. An agent can call a seller’s website directly, but every request shows its IP address, and to get a reply later it needs an account – a registration that is hard for an agent, and that identifies the buyer. And it still finds sellers through search that big tech owns.

A public square. Rosenblat, Strauss and O’Reilly propose a public space where agents broadcast their requests, and sellers’ agents answer6. But broadcasting makes every request public, so buyers can no longer ask without being identified. The authors list privacy loss among the risks themselves. It should be the opposite: offers are public, and requests are private.

We built SimpleX for people – it is ready for agents

We have been building SimpleX since 2020 for private communication between people. It is the only messaging network without any user identifiers7: nobody can contact you, or pretend to be somebody you know, unless you give them a link. Over 600,000 people use it every month.

SimpleX can enable an open market for agents. Sellers would publish their addresses under SimpleX names that only they control8. Buyers’ agents would read sellers’ channels privately9 and contact each seller from a throwaway profile with a hidden IP address. And nobody sits in between, including us10: the servers relay encrypted messages without knowing who sends them to whom.

Some businesses already support their customers via SimpleX. Test names work now, and .simplex names go on sale on December 12, 2026. We have just released an MCP server11 that connects any agent to SimpleX, and published open specifications for websites and agents12.

What the market can become

On this network, the market can open again: a buyer’s agent compares thousands of offers, asks the best sellers privately, and buys with no platform taking a cut. To get there, we are building:

  • catalogs of sellers on SimpleX names, curated by people and AI-powered services;
  • agents calling each other’s tools with a request over SimpleX, without creating a conversation;
  • custom interfaces inside chats; and
  • an autonomous namespace, so that no company, including ours, can take a seller’s name away.
